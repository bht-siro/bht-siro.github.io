Teaching

At the SIRo Lab, teaching is not a side activity but one of the settings in which our research is applied, alongside greenhouse work, ambulatory care and the reprocessing of surgical instruments. Students build the hands and operate the robots we do research with.

Research platforms are student platforms

The lab is embedded in BHT’s B.Sc. Humanoid Robotics, one of the few programmes in Germany dedicated to humanoid robotics. The platforms students work with from the second semester are the same ones we use in research: the same compliant hands, the same TIAGo Pro, the same challenges.

The boundary between teaching and research is deliberately thin. Student prototypes regularly become lab platforms, and active research questions flow straight into lectures and projects.

The Robotics Project

In the Robotics Project, student teams design, build, and test a soft robotic hand from scratch, then prove it in a yearly challenge. The tasks are not invented for the classroom. They come either from an external benchmark or from our own research.

In 2023 the teams worked through the task catalogue of the Cybathlon Arm Prosthesis Race unchanged: grasping and lifting objects of different size and weight, handling compliant objects such as clothing, and two-handed tasks such as zipping and hanging. In 2024 they moved into the BHT greenhouse, where each team had to build a hand together with a controller small enough to be held in one hand, then de-sucker and defoliate tomato plants, pick single fruits and cut whole clusters, all operated single-handedly. Those greenhouse tasks are the same ones we address in our DFG tele.interaction project, and the one-handed controller poses the same question as our input station, at a scale a student team can build in a semester.

TIAGo Pro also works in the courses themselves, as a scrum master and in the ball pointing game. That is the robot used as a tool in a setting and is described under applications.

How the teaching is set up

None of this is incidental. We deliberately put research hardware in front of students early, set tasks that come from outside the classroom, and let student work feed back into the lab. The reasoning is simple: a task with a real constraint, a tomato that bruises or a benchmark someone else wrote, cannot be satisfied by a plausible-looking report. It either works or it does not, and that is what makes it worth a semester.

The same decision runs the other way. Student prototypes that hold up become lab platforms, and open research questions become project topics rather than staying in the group.
