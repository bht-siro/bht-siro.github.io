Researcher

Felix Schlüter

How does someone with no training in operating a humanoid control it with ease, and feel what the robot feels?

A low-cost input device for teleoperation, and robotic assistance in ambulatory care.

Felix.Schlueter@bht-berlin.de

Research focus

Felix works in Robo4Care, the project that asks what a robot can realistically take over in ambulatory care. Preparing medication is one of the candidate tasks: it is repetitive, it involves small and easily damaged objects, and it has to be reliable. That combination is what compliant hands and a human in the loop are meant to address.

His technical contribution is the input device. He develops a station in the spirit of GELLO, a teleoperation approach in which the operator moves a small replica of the robot arm: the input device shares the kinematic structure of the target arm and is built from 3D-printed parts and inexpensive motors.

It deliberately does without force feedback. What it offers instead is a direct mapping and a low barrier to use, because the operator moves a scaled copy of the machine being commanded. The principle follows GELLO, a low-cost teleoperation framework from UC Berkeley in which the input device shares the kinematic structure of the target arm and is built from 3D-printed parts and off-the-shelf motors. The device is nearly finished and not yet in use; the aim is to have it working by the end of the year. It is meant to complement the lab’s visuo-haptic input station: the haptic station investigates how contact should be conveyed back to an operator, while this device is built to make teleoperation practical for everyday use, for collecting demonstrations and for running the care scenarios.

Both sides meet in the care setting: a straightforward way to command the robot, and hands soft enough for what care work actually involves.
