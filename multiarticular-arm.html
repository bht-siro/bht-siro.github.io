Multiarticular VSA Arm

What becomes possible when elasticity spans more than one joint?

Variable stiffness across coupled joints

We develop a two-degree-of-freedom robot arm with variable-stiffness actuation (VSA) and an elastic coupling that spans both joints. The approach is inspired by biarticular muscles, such as the biceps, which transfer energy between joints and influence how a limb responds to external forces.

Adjustable stiffness at individual joints limits the combinations of end-effector stiffness that an arm can achieve. Biarticular couplings add off-diagonal terms to the joint stiffness matrix, extending that range. We investigate how these couplings can shape the arm’s response to contact and support dynamic movements.

Monoarticular only

Each joint has independent actuation and elasticity. The joint stiffness matrix is diagonal, limiting the achievable combinations of stiffness at the end-effector.

With biarticular couplings

Across a million Cartesian stiffness combinations evaluated for a two-joint arm model, the monoarticular version reached 0.2 per cent of them and the biarticular version 14.5 per cent: a 70-fold gain in versatility, measured as the range of achievable ellipse shapes and orientations.

Figures from Höppner, Wiedmeyer and van der Smagt, ICRA 2014. That work was carried out at the German Aerospace Center (DLR), before the SIRo Lab existed.

Shaping end-effector stiffness

In a planar model, a stiffness ellipse describes how strongly the arm resists displacement in different directions. A longer principal axis represents greater stiffness in that direction. The corresponding compliance, the inverse of stiffness, describes deflection under force. We aim to adjust the ellipse’s shape, size and orientation to match the requirements of a task.

Shape
Directional response

An elongated ellipse indicates different stiffness along its principal axes; a circle represents equal stiffness in all planar directions.

Size
Overall stiffness

The overall resistance to displacement, while retaining the relative stiffness between directions.

Orientation
Aligned to the task

The directions in which the arm should resist displacement or yield during contact.

Research objectives

We investigate the following potential benefits of coupled elasticity. Their performance on the complete arm remains to be evaluated.

Contact-guided insertion. Compliance across an insertion axis may allow contact to correct small alignment errors, while stiffness along the axis supports insertion. A key and lock provide a simple example of this task-dependent response.

Directional guidance during impact. For tasks such as hammering, we investigate whether high lateral stiffness can limit sideways motion while allowing movement along the direction of impact.

Interaction between mechanics and control. Aligning the principal directions of stiffness and inertia may simplify control. We aim to quantify the effect across arm configurations.

Energy transfer between joints. Biarticular couplings can redistribute stored elastic energy during movement. We investigate their potential to support dynamic tasks such as throwing and catching.

Open research questions

We use the platform to examine where coupled elasticity and adjustable stiffness justify the additional mechanical complexity.

  • Coupling arrangement. Which pairs of joints should be coupled, and how does their placement affect the achievable end-effector stiffness?
  • Actuator selection. Where is adjustable stiffness beneficial, and where is a simpler series-elastic actuator sufficient?
  • Mechanical and controlled compliance. Which responses should be provided by passive elasticity, and which require active control?
  • Spatial mechanisms. How do the findings from planar models extend to arms with three-dimensional motion and redundant joints?

The two-degree-of-freedom arm is an independent research platform for elastic humanoid limbs and is not mounted on TIAGo Pro. It has been built and measured several times; final evaluation of those measurements is pending.

Published foundations and current development

The platform builds on a biarticular mechanism developed by Prof. Höppner at DLR in 2014. It uses springs and cam discs to extend the achievable stiffness range. The 70-fold increase reported above refers to that study.

The 2014 work was carried out at the German Aerospace Center (DLR), before the SIRo Lab was established.

At SIRo Lab, we developed 3D-printed nonlinear torsional springs (3DNS), published in IEEE RA-L in 2025. Their geometry produces a progressive stiffness characteristic as sections of the spring come into contact. The paper evaluates individual springs and a single antagonistic variable-stiffness joint through static and dynamic measurements. That published joint does not implement bidirectional antagonism; the current two-degree-of-freedom arm extends the work to coupled joints.

Separately, we have built a first one-degree-of-freedom prototype of a bidirectional antagonistic variable-stiffness actuator (BAVS). This actuator is distinct from the coupled arm, and the prototype work is unpublished. The BAVS control paper from 2022 concerns earlier experiments on the DLR David forearm joint.
