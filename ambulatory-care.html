Ambulatory Care

Care work is under pressure on two fronts at once: delicate fine-motor tasks and physically demanding ones. We address both.

Relieving care on two fronts

In Robo4Care, an IFAF Berlin project, we tackle the strain on care staff from two directions. Some tasks are delicate and repetitive; others are heavy and ergonomically harmful. A single technology rarely fits both, so we pair compliant manipulation with wearable assistance.

1

Compliant manipulation

A TIAGo Pro with SIRo Soft Hands is the platform for the question we actually work on: which fine-motor care tasks a compliant, teleoperated system can take on at all, and where the limits are. Together with our practice partners we are assessing candidate tasks, from handling small objects and preparing medication to routine measurements. Which of them prove feasible, and under what supervision, is the outcome of the project, not its premise.

We research hardware and teleoperation. The lab does not develop medical devices and makes no diagnostic claims.

2

Exoskeletons

For physically demanding work like patient transfers, wearable exoskeletons reduce the ergonomic load on the carer’s back and joints. We evaluate and help optimise them in real care settings.

A real-world consortium

Care cannot be studied in the lab alone, so Robo4Care is built together with the people who do the work. It brings together three Berlin universities of applied sciences with a practice partner and ergonomics experts, running from 2026 to 2027.

IFAF Berlin · 2026–2027 ASH Berlin HTW Berlin BHT Johannesstift Diakonie IFA / DGUV Fraunhofer IZM / IPK

ASH Berlin contributes nursing-science expertise, HTW Berlin the measurement and exoskeleton optimisation, and BHT the robotics and exoskeleton evaluation. Johannesstift Diakonie provides the care settings where the work is tested.

The research question

Assistance in care is only useful if it reduces the workload without adding friction to the workflow. We study where compliant teleoperation supports a caregiver in everyday tasks, and where a wearable system reduces strain enough to make a difference, assessed together with the people doing the work rather than only on their behalf.
