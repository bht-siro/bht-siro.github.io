Prof. Dr.-Ing. Hannes Höppner

Compliant hands and arms, the measurement of human stiffness, and the transfer of both to robots.

hannes.hoeppner@bht-berlin.de · +49 30 4504-2357 · ORCID 0000-0003-3184-7653 · Google Scholar · ResearchGate · BHT profile

Profile

Hannes Höppner has been Professor for Humanoid Robotics at Berliner Hochschule für Technik since October 2019 and heads the Soft Interactive Robotics Laboratory. The lab studies compliant hands and arms, teleoperation with haptic feedback, and the acquisition of manipulation skills from human demonstration. He also runs the teaching laboratory for Humanoid Robotics at BHT.

His research interests centre on the design and application of soft robotic hands, on continuous and perturbation-based methods for measuring the intrinsic properties of the human hand and arm, on transferring those properties to robots through teleoperation, and on the design of variable stiffness actuators for arms and hands. A further strand concerns safety systems for close collaboration between people and robots.

Before BHT he led sports technology development at the Institute of Applied Training Science in Leipzig, and spent nine years as a researcher at the German Aerospace Center (DLR) in the Institute of Robotics and Mechatronics, latterly as principal investigator for the EU project soma. He received his doctorate from the Technical University of Munich in 2016.

Teaching

In the Bachelor’s programme Humanoid Robotics at BHT he teaches subjects around the mechanical design, manufacture and maintenance of robotic systems, as well as human-robot interaction.

The Robotics Project is the most visible of these. Second-semester students develop a robotic hand from scratch, in groups of four, and mount it on a Franka Emika Panda arm to pick up a set of awkward objects and place them at a distance: a business card, a slice of toast, a net of limes, a wooden plate, an ice cream cone, a paper cup. Each group receives five Dynamixel servos, the necessary electronics and a coupling, and builds the hand using 3D printing, laser cutting, vacuum thermoforming and casting of polyurethane in custom moulds. A Python API lets them close the fingers until contact. The results are consistently impressive: all hands are cable-driven, many can actively oppose a thumb, and some have two.
