VHIS: Visuo-Haptic Input Station

How does someone with no training in operating a humanoid control it with ease, and feel what the robot feels?

Concept

The VHIS (Visuo-Haptic Input Station) is a dedicated input station that allows an operator to teleoperate a humanoid robot (in our case, a TIAGo Pro) through natural, bimanual arm and hand movements. Built around two Franka Research 3 (FR3) cobot arms, the station captures both visual and haptic information, transmitting the operator’s intent while providing real-time sensory feedback from the remote environment.

Unlike conventional joystick or keyboard interfaces, the VHIS is designed to preserve the spatial structure of human manipulation. The operator’s arm poses are mapped onto the robot’s workspace, and the haptic channel is meant to carry contact forces, stiffness and surface properties back, so that what arrives is interaction rather than remote pose commanding.

Input Modality
Dual-Arm Haptic

Two Franka Research 3 (FR3) cobot arms act as both input (tracking the operator’s motion) and output (rendering haptic feedback)

Visual Feedback
Immersive Stereo

Stereo camera feed from the robot’s perspective, displayed in a head-mounted or screen-based viewer

Optimisation
Spatial Configuration

Cobot placement optimised with continuous reach-envelope meshes, volumetric intersection analysis and two new metrics (Arm Closeness Index, Joint Closeness Index); one arm optimised, the second mirrored

Target Robot
TIAGo Pro

PAL Robotics’ dual-arm mobile humanoid, the robot the station is being built to command

Where the station stands today

The station is a research platform under construction, and the three parts of it are at very different stages. We separate them here because the difference matters more than the vision does.

  • published Where the arms belong. How to place a cobot base next to an operator so that reachable and dexterous workspace are maximised, with continuous reach-envelope meshes and volumetric intersection analysis; the second arm is mirrored. This is the one result that has been through review (Humanoids 2025).
  • shown internally Commanding a robot arm. First demonstrations of teleoperating one TIAGo arm from a Franka arm. Not published, not evaluated, and shown with a single arm rather than the full dual-arm station.
  • shown once Feeling what the robot feels. Haptic feedback to the operator has been demonstrated once. What it can convey reliably, and whether it changes what a non-expert can do, is exactly what is still open.
  • planned The whole loop. Both arms, hands on the robot, contact conveyed as contact, tested with people who are not roboticists.

Design Approach

A station like this only works if the cobot stands in the right place next to the operator. Placed badly, it costs the operator reach, or it runs into its own joint limits partway through a motion. The published result is a way of deriving that placement rather than settling it by intuition. One arm is optimised and the second is mirrored. The steps below are how it is done; the method and the figures are from the Humanoids 2025 paper.

Describe both arms in the same terms

One side is a person, the other is a Franka Research 3. Both are written as a seven-joint kinematic chain, so that the two can be compared at all. The human side has a twist: its joint limits are not fixed numbers but depend on how the arm is currently held, because muscles and tendons couple the joints to one another.

Work out what each of them can reach

The usual way to map a human workspace is to ask, for many points in space, whether the arm can reach them: inverse kinematics. It gives an answer for every point and a posture that no shoulder would actually adopt. So the reachable space is built the other way round, by sweeping the joints through their allowed ranges and recording where the hand ends up. That yields about 312,000 poses; discarding everything behind the shoulder, everything that collides with head or torso and everything below the hip leaves roughly 83,000, and the outer shell of those becomes a closed mesh. A volume, not a cloud of points, which is what makes the next step possible.

On the robot side each point carries a number rather than a yes or no: the average manipulability over 216 orientations, so the model knows not only where the arm reaches but how freely it can move once it is there.

Score every candidate placement, then look at it

Because the two workspaces are volumes, a candidate placement can be scored by how much of the operator’s space the robot’s space covers. That is cheap enough to run over 729,000 candidate poses around the operator, and the ones reaching at least 90 per cent overlap go forward as starting points. From there the search maximises manipulability across the operator’s workspace and returns 200 refined placements.

Two further measures then separate them. Joint closeness asks how far the robot’s joints sit from their limits: a placement can score high on manipulability and still leave a joint a few degrees short of its stop, where one unexpected operator movement triggers a protective halt in the middle of a task. Arm closeness asks how much room the operator has left, measured as the volume spanned between the person’s arm and the robot’s. Larger is better. Both are new here, and they put the station on the same axes as comparable setups from other groups.

Two weeks of computation on a cluster narrow 729,000 candidates down to five. Looking at those five then removes three of them, because they put the arm where the person’s own arm needs to be, which no index in the set was measuring. Two remain for testing on the real station.

This is the part of the station that has been through review. Everything downstream of it, commanding the robot and feeling what it touches, is at the earlier stages listed above.

Research Questions

The VHIS is the central hardware platform for the DFG-funded tele.interaction project (Großgeräteinitiative HAW, project number 533327659, 2024–2028). The research investigates:

  • Spatial configuration: How should the input station’s kinematic structure be arranged to maximise the operator’s reachable and dexterous workspace?
  • Manipulability transfer: How do the manipulability characteristics of the input device map onto those of the remote robot, and how does this affect task performance?
  • Interaction quality: What metrics capture the quality of remote physical interaction (grasping, assembly, palpation), and how can the station be optimised for them?
  • Teleimpedance: Can the operator’s arm and hand impedance (stiffness, damping) be measured in real-time and transferred to the robot for compliant, adaptive remote manipulation?

Context: From DLR to BHT

The VHIS builds on a lineage of teleimpedance and visuo-haptic research. During his time at the German Aerospace Center (DLR, 2009–2018), Prof. Höppner developed methods for measuring human arm and hand stiffness during interaction, and for transferring these impedance profiles to robotic systems, a concept known as teleimpedance.

At BHT, this concept is extended to a full-body humanoid teleoperation scenario. The VHIS is designed to close the loop between the operator and the TIAGo Pro, enabling not just position control but impedance-aware interaction across distance.
